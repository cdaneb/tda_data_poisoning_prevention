\begin{abstract}
Topological data analysis (TDA) has been proposed as a filter for detecting poisoned
records in machine-learning network intrusion detection. We audit such a pipeline at
five levels---reproduction, representation, cluster separability, label-free removal,
and downstream classifier utility---using UNSW-NB15 and CICIDS2017 Payload-Byte data.
We show that the poison--unmodified feature collisions that defeat the filter are
largely a property of payload duplication and binarization rather than of persistent
homology, and that a fixed nine-threshold representation removes 92.0--95.9\%
of exact matches. That reduction does not produce consistent poison removal or
classifier benefit. Fixed OPTICS removal stays low; a prespecified confirmation
study spanning 90 experimental configurations
finds no general advantage for the 540-feature representation; and a
preregistered downstream study spanning 45 configurations and six training-condition
types satisfies only two of four prespecified criteria on UNSW and shows no filter-specific benefit on
either CICIDS population. Results suggest that TDA filtering yields a dataset-specific enrichment
signal rather than demonstrated operational value, and success at one evaluation level does
not establish the next.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
data poisoning, network intrusion detection, persistent homology, topological data analysis
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
Training-data integrity is a security requirement for machine-learning network
intrusion detection systems. A poisoning adversary can corrupt historical
traffic so that a learned detector acquires an attacker-chosen weakness
~\cite{biggio2018}. Training-data integrity is a veracity problem at scale: the
filter must decide which of millions of unlabeled historical records to discard,
using no poison labels and at a bounded cost in discarded legitimate traffic.

Monkam, De Lucia, and Bastian proposed mapping packet payloads to
persistence-derived features and clustering them before model
training~\cite{monkam2024}. Persistent homology compresses connected
components and loops across filtrations~\cite{carlsson2009,cohensteiner2007},
but failed capture can arise from the input data, binarization, topological
summary, or cluster geometry. A final score alone cannot identify the cause.

This paper asks four linked questions: what the supplied implementation
reproduces; at which stage exact poison--unmodified equivalences first arise;
whether repairing those equivalences improves label-free removal at controlled
unmodified-data cost; and whether removal measurably benefits a downstream
classifier evaluated on test payloads absent from training. The supplied DoS examples and controlled permutation
experiments remain separate threat models, and neither is an exact reproduction
of Ref.~\cite{monkam2024}. This paper contributes: (i) a forensic audit of supplied
126- and 280-feature artifacts; (ii) an audit of the first stage that creates
exact feature matches and a fixed nine-threshold representation; (iii)
classifier evaluation with exact-duplicate groups kept within one data split;
(iv) novelty detection fitted and calibrated on trusted unmodified data, with
independent prespecified confirmation on UNSW and CICIDS; and (v) a downstream
evaluation preregistered on the Open Science Framework (OSF), with six training-condition types including
equal-cost random removal and perfect poison-identification controls.
We use \emph{poison} for deliberately
manipulated training observations; the term does not assume that every such
observation produces practically material classifier damage.


\section{Background and Related Work}
\subsection{Dataset and supplied artifacts}
UNSW-NB15 is a widely used intrusion-detection benchmark containing normal
traffic and nine attack categories~\cite{moustafa2015}. This work uses only the
packet-level Payload-Byte derivative produced by the extraction and labeling
procedure of Farrukh \emph{et al.}~\cite{farrukh2022}: 79,881 padded
1,500-byte packet records, not the original flow-feature table. The confirmation
study uses a pre-existing 1,410,255-row CICIDS2017 CSV~\cite{sharafaldin2018} in
the same 1,500-byte Payload-Byte schema~\cite{farrukh2022}. This study did not
regenerate that derivative from packet captures; its exact extraction
provenance beyond the schema and frozen file hash is therefore unverified.

The authors of Ref.~\cite{monkam2024} supplied the audited package, which contains six
Jupyter notebooks, nineteen CSV files, two Excel
feature tables, and an archive. Its poisoning path uses a $1\times1500$ raster,
usually threshold 0.3, and 72/126 features; Algorithm~1 of Ref.~\cite{monkam2024}
instead states 0.4.
Only 18 of 1,000 saved poison examples and only the density-based spatial
clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) configuration are available.
The generator and selected settings for hierarchical DBSCAN (HDBSCAN), Ordering
Points To Identify the Clustering Structure (OPTICS), and Mean Shift are missing.

\subsection{Representation and evaluation}
Persistence diagrams record feature births and deaths; finite summaries enable
machine learning but are not injective~\cite{cohensteiner2007,tauzin2021}.
Raster geometry and fitted preprocessing also matter. A $1\times1500$ cubical
complex has no nontrivial one-dimensional homology ($H_1$) loops. Moreover, \texttt{Binarizer} learns a
batch maximum and \texttt{Scaler} a diagram scale, so separate batch fits can
create incompatible coordinates.

Exact raw payloads and TDA vectors also repeat. Random row splits can place
identical observations in both training and test data; grouping by exact
identity keeps every duplicate group entirely within one split.
Finally, OPTICS may label both poison and unmodified rows as noise
~\cite{ankerst1999}. Cluster purity is therefore retrospective. Operational
evaluation instead fits and calibrates novelty scores on disjoint trusted
unmodified subsets and reports poison capture with realized held-out
unmodified removal.

\subsection{Poisoning defenses and data sanitization}
Training-data sanitization predates TDA-based filtering. Early work treated
sanitization as an anomaly-sensor problem, iteratively identifying training
points that distorted a learned model~\cite{cretu2008}, while Reject on
Negative Impact (RONI)
screening rejected candidates whose inclusion had a negative impact on a
trusted validation objective~\cite{nelson2008}. Label sanitization similarly
uses trusted structure to identify likely label-flipping attacks
~\cite{paudice2018}. These methods differ in score construction and threat
model, but all ask whether suspect observations can be removed before they
corrupt training.

Representation-space defenses make this separability assumption explicit.
Spectral signatures search learned features for directions associated with a
backdoor~\cite{tran2018}; activation clustering partitions internal
representations to expose poisoned subpopulations~\cite{chen2019}; and deep
$k$-nearest-neighbor (kNN) filtering rejects clean-label poisons using neighborhood inconsistency
~\cite{peri2020}. Each assumes that poison is separable in some learned
representation. The present paper tests that assumption for a
persistence-derived representation and then asks whether any separation
survives label-free calibration and improves a downstream classifier.
Certified defenses and robust objectives form a distinct branch: they seek
worst-case guarantees under explicit contamination assumptions rather than a
retrospective anomaly score~\cite{steinhardt2017}. Their guarantees depend on
the stated threat set and need not predict empirical separability under the
packet representation studied here. This audit adds a
cost-matched, preregistered evaluation on test payloads whose exact identities
are excluded from training, testing whether
representation-level separation translates to classifier-level utility, a
link that these defense studies generally do not isolate.

\subsection{Forensic and faithful-representation audits}
This audit determines which supplied artifacts can support faithful reconstruction.
Files, Jupyter notebook execution, sample construction, fit boundaries, and clustering
calls were inventoried. The supplied Excel feature tables were checked for
constant coordinates, exact classes, label conflicts, and raw alignment. The
reconstructed 126-feature extraction pipeline uses seven filtrations,
zero- and one-dimensional homology ($H_0$ and $H_1$), and nine summaries on a
$1\times1500$ raster; the 280-feature
Jupyter notebook configuration uses 20 summaries without persistence-diagram
scaling. Synthetic persistence diagrams verified feature order. The supplied 280-feature Excel table aligns
with the raw-data rows, whereas the 31,000-row 126-feature Excel table does not
because 10,000 rows were sampled without a recorded random seed.

A prespecified comparison using random seed 60 then changes only how the
126-feature pipeline is fitted: separate poison/unmodified fits versus one
combined fit. The comparison uses all 18 delivered poisons, 378 normal and 180
unmodified attack-category rows, a fixed 50\% split, threshold 0.3, and DBSCAN
(\texttt{eps}$=200$,
\texttt{min\_samples}$=9$). This preserves the Jupyter notebook's $21{:}10$
unmodified composition and 3.125\% poison share. Learned binarization cutoffs,
persistence-diagram scales, feature displacement, silhouette, adjusted Rand
index (ARI), cluster composition, and removal are recorded.
Expansion to multiple random seeds requires both different fitted preprocessing
states and a material change in cluster assignments.

\subsection{Controlled reconstruction and experimental frames}
The two frames separate artifact diagnosis from attack-specific evaluation.
Each seed in $\{42,123,456,789,1024\}$ uses 5,000 unmodified rows plus 500
poisoned variants. Here \emph{unmodified} is not necessarily benign; poison
sources carry attack labels. Frame~A, the permissive transposition audit,
evaluates the existing single-threshold pipeline with 60 transpositions and
permits attacks that leave the raw payload unchanged. Frame~A is therefore diagnostic of the
representation under a permissive attack draw, not of any single adversary.
Frame~B, the guaranteed raw-changing attack evaluation, redraws attack
parameters until every poison differs from its source, so results do not
transfer between frames.

Frame~B defines the active payload region to end one position beyond the last
nonzero byte; attacks act only inside that region. They are 60 disjoint transpositions, one
120-byte reversal, two swapped 60-byte blocks, or a nonzero cyclic shift.
Each of 20 attack-family-by-seed configurations contains 500 raw-changing poisons.

These families are a worst-case probe of the representation, not a claim about
typical adversary behavior. A defender cannot assume that an adversary is
unaware of the feature-extraction pipeline: permutation-preserving edits are the cheapest edit
class an adversary reading the equivariance relation of Section~III-C would select. Preserving
the byte multiset also holds downstream label-relevant content approximately
fixed, isolating the representation effect from the unrelated confound of a
poison that is merely out of distribution. This design choice limits realism. The
families do not span realistic protocol-valid mutations, and the negative
results are strongest as statements about this adversary class; as noted in
the Limitations, functional packet validity is not established.

The controlled reconstruction reshapes payloads to $30\times50$, binarizes at
$t=0.4$ (byte cut 102), and applies two height and three radial filtrations.
Cubical persistence, diagram scaling, entropy, and five amplitudes over
$H_0/H_1$ produce 60 features. OPTICS uses
\texttt{min\_samples}$=5$, \texttt{max\_eps}$=2.0$, and standard
$\xi$ extraction. All settings were fixed before evaluation.

\subsection{First-stage attribution of exact matches (Frame~A)}
This audit locates the first pipeline stage at which final-vector collisions arise.
Exact signatures are computed at the raw payload, recorded active-payload
boundary, binary mask,
filtrations, unscaled/scaled diagrams, summaries, and final vector. Diagram
points are canonically ordered without float rounding. Each repeated final
member is assigned to the earliest stage where it joins a group of at least two
identical signatures. These stage categories are mutually exclusive, and the
signature groups merge but never split in every seed.
Shares use repeated final-vector members as denominator, not all 5,500 rows.
A separate outcome is the fraction of poisoned rows whose final feature vector
exactly matches an unmodified row's vector.

If $\phi_k$ is the signature at stage $k$, define
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
 i\sim_k j &\iff \phi_k(x_i)=\phi_k(x_j),\\
 e(i) &=\min\{k:|[i]_{\sim_k}|>1\}.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:earliest}
\end{equation}
For the set $M$ of repeated final-vector members, the stage-$k$ attribution is
$|\{i\in M:e(i)=k\}|/|M|$. This construction makes the reported percentages a
partition of collision mass rather than overlapping duplicate counts.

The targeted representation weakness follows directly from the permutation
action. Let $P_\sigma$ permute pixel positions and let
$B_t(\vect{x})_j=\mathbf{1}\{x_j>255t\}$. Then
\begin{equation}
 B_t(P_\sigma\vect{x})=P_\sigma B_t(\vect{x}), \qquad
 \|B_t(P_\sigma\vect{x})\|_1=\|B_t(\vect{x})\|_1 .
 \label{eq:invariance}
\end{equation}
Permutation preserves the fitted maximum and foreground count. If moved bytes
remain within threshold classes, the whole mask is unchanged. Equation
~\eqref{eq:invariance} is equivariance, not invariance of later
position-dependent filtrations.

\subsection{Fixed nine-threshold representation}
\label{sec:stack}
This intervention tests whether fixed multithresholding reduces exact feature-vector matches.
The only intervention replaces $t=0.4$ with the fixed threshold set
$T=\{0.1,0.2,\ldots,0.9\}$. For every threshold, the unchanged pipeline emits a
60-feature vector $\vect{f}_t(\vect{x})$; the nine-threshold representation is
\begin{equation}
 \vect{F}(\vect{x})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{9}}
 [\vect{f}_{0.1}(\vect{x}),\ldots,\vect{f}_{0.9}(\vect{x})]\in\mathbb{R}^{540}.
 \label{eq:stack}
\end{equation}
The fixed factor $1/\sqrt{9}$ preserves Euclidean distance scale if all nine
blocks were identical. Blocks have equal, fixed weights. The $t=0.4$ block from
the same poisoned dataset must exactly match the 60-feature single-threshold
baseline used in the experiment.

\subsection{Evaluation protocols}
The protocols separate retrospective cluster purity from label-free operational removal.
Retrospective OPTICS removal selects non-noise clusters above poison-purity
cutoffs $q\in\{1.0,>0.95,>0.90,>0.80,>0.50\}$. Let
$R_p^a(q)$ and $R_c^a(q)$ be the fractions of poison and unmodified rows
removed using representation $a$. Superscripts $\mathrm{ctl}$ and
$\mathrm{stk}$ denote the single-threshold baseline and nine-threshold
representation, respectively. At each purity cutoff for the baseline, the
cost-matched improvement in poison removal is
\begin{equation}
 \Delta(q)=
 \max_{\substack{q':\,R_c^{\mathrm{stk}}(q')\\
                  \le R_c^{\mathrm{ctl}}(q)}}
 \left[R_p^{\mathrm{stk}}(q')-R_p^{\mathrm{ctl}}(q)\right].
 \label{eq:matched}
\end{equation}
Thus the nine-threshold representation receives no more unmodified-row removal
cost than the single-threshold baseline; exact feature matching and OPTICS
noise rates are secondary outcomes.

On all 79,881 aligned rows, five-seed 100-tree random forests compare raw bytes
with the supplied 280-feature TDA representation for ten-class (20\% test) and
binary (10\% test) tasks. Random-row splits are compared with group splits that
keep every exact raw-payload identity or exact TDA-vector identity entirely
within training or test; conflicting labels remain. The model seed equals the split seed.

For label-free detection, Frame~B unmodified rows are split by exact raw-payload identity
into 60\% training, 20\% calibration, and 20\% evaluation. Training-only
standardization precedes 10-neighbor distance, 20-neighbor novelty local
outlier factor (LOF), 200-tree Isolation Forest, and a radial-basis-function
(RBF) one-class support vector machine (SVM)
($\nu=0.01$). Calibration quantiles set nominal $0.1,0.5,1,2,5\%$
unmodified-row removal targets. No poison label fits a
detector or threshold; capture is reported with realized held-out unmodified
removal, precision, and areas under the receiver-operating-characteristic and
precision--recall curves (AUROC and AUPRC).

Formally, let $\mathcal C$, $\mathcal U$, and $\mathcal P$ denote calibration
unmodified, held-out unmodified, and poisoned sets, and let larger $s(x)$ mean
more anomalous. For nominal removal target $b$, the calibration-only threshold and
reported operating point are
\begin{align}
 \tau_b &= Q_{1-b}\{s(x):x\in\mathcal C\}, \nonumber\\
 C_p(b) &= |\{x\in\mathcal P:s(x)>\tau_b\}|/|\mathcal P|, \nonumber\\
 C_u(b) &= |\{x\in\mathcal U:s(x)>\tau_b\}|/|\mathcal U|.
 \label{eq:novelty}
\end{align}
$C_p$ is poison capture and $C_u$ is realized unmodified removal. Reporting
both prevents the calibration removal target from being mistaken for its
held-out cost.

After exploratory analysis was complete, an independent prespecified
confirmation study fixed Isolation Forest as primary, kNN as secondary, seeds
2026--2030, and nominal 1\% and 5\% unmodified-row removal targets. The
unseen-seed UNSW and matched-size CICIDS experiments repeat all four attack
families with 5,000 unmodified and 500 poisoned rows; the large-scale CICIDS
experiment uses 50,000 unmodified and 5,000 transposition-poisoned rows. Its
prespecified criteria are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(P1)] unseen-seed UNSW mean paired capture difference between Isolation
Forest on the nine-threshold 540-feature representation (IF-540) and on the
single-threshold 60-feature baseline (IF-60) is positive;
\item[(P2)] its paired 95\% bootstrap interval excludes zero;
\item[(P3)] unseen-seed UNSW mean IF-540 capture is at least 12\%;
\item[(P4)] unseen-seed UNSW mean IF-540 unmodified removal is 4--6\%;
\item[(T1)] matched-size CICIDS mean IF-540 unmodified removal is 4--6\%;
\item[(T2)] matched-size CICIDS mean paired IF-540-minus-IF-60 capture is positive;
\item[(T3)] large-scale CICIDS mean IF-540 unmodified removal is 4--6\%.
\end{enumerate}
kNN carries no separate pass/fail criterion and cannot rescue a failed primary
claim; it is reported as prespecified secondary evidence.

\subsection{Preregistered downstream classifier evaluation}
The final experiment asks whether filtering changes classifier behavior, rather
than only whether it removes manipulated rows. Before observing these outcomes,
the full design was registered at \url{https://osf.io/h863t/}.
The downstream evaluation reuses the five prespecified confirmation seeds,
2026--2030, and their unmodified-data partitions, applying the same sanitizer
design before measuring the new outcome of downstream classifier behavior.
Each seed uses the four Frame~B families on matched-size UNSW and CICIDS populations
(5,000 unmodified plus 500 poison); the large-scale CICIDS experiment uses transpositions with
50,000 plus 5,000, for 45 population--seed--attack-family configurations. Poison copies are
generated from malicious-class parents but receive a benign training label.

The six training-condition types are clean (unpoisoned) training, poisoned training without
filtering, random removal matched exactly to the detector's unmodified-row
cost, IF-60 removal, IF-540 removal, and oracle removal that identifies all
poisoned rows. Instantiating the filter and random-removal types for two
representations and two removal targets yields 11 arms per configuration:
clean, poisoned, oracle, four filter arms, and four paired random-removal arms.
Detector fitting and calibration use disjoint trusted
unmodified subsets; filtering acts only on the suspect subset. The downstream
poison-capture rate divides by all poisoned rows. Table~\ref{tab:confirmation}
reports realized unmodified removal relative to the held-out unmodified rows in
that suspect subset; Section~IV-H and Fig.~\ref{fig:downstream-generalization}
additionally express the same removed-row count relative to all unmodified
training rows. The downstream
model is a 100-tree random forest on raw bytes, with the random seed assigned to
that experimental configuration as
\texttt{random\_state}, no preprocessing, and otherwise default
scikit-learn settings. Each test set is unmodified, equal in size to its
training population, and excludes every exact raw-payload identity appearing in
any paired training condition. The primary endpoint is malicious recall; benign
false-positive rate (FPR) is the safety constraint.

The sole primary analysis is matched-size UNSW, IF-60, and the 5\% calibration
removal target. An operational-benefit claim requires all four criteria: the
mean recall difference between clean and poisoned training is at least 0.02 and
the lower bound of its confidence interval (CI) exceeds zero; the mean recall
difference between IF-60 filtering and poisoned training is at least 0.01 and
its CI lower bound exceeds zero; IF-60 recall exceeds recall after equal-cost
random removal with a CI lower bound above zero; and the CI upper bound for the
benign-FPR difference between IF-60 filtering and clean training is at most
0.01. Paired 95\% intervals use
100,000 hierarchical bootstrap draws (seed 20260904), resampling seeds and then
families. IF-540, the 1\% removal target, CICIDS, attacked-test copies, and all other
metrics are prespecified but cannot rescue a failed primary claim.

\subsection{Reproducibility}
The reproducibility controls bind every reported result to a frozen design and artifact.
Each study has an immutable specification and machine-readable artifacts.
Acceptance checks verify sample counts, zero Frame~B attacks that leave the raw
payload unchanged, finite 60/540 features, equality between the 60-feature
baseline and its corresponding block in the 540-feature vector, monotonic
stage-wise collision classes, and zero train--test group overlap.
Settings and endpoints were fixed before each full run. The confirmation adds
literal row/index hashes, checks that fitting and calibration use only trusted
unmodified data, attack-parent
reproduction, finite score checks, and completeness of every experimental
configuration; all 90 configurations pass. One configuration is a
population--attack-family--seed--representation combination:
$4\times5\times2=40$ for unseen-seed UNSW, 40 for matched-size CICIDS, and
$1\times5\times2=10$ for large-scale CICIDS. Both detectors and removal targets are evaluated
within each configuration. The downstream manifest was frozen before model outcomes,
bound to registration hash
\texttt{3451b9ee\ldots 105ade}, and executed only after the OSF receipt was
recorded. All 45 experimental configurations and 495 training-condition metric
rows pass checks of content hashes, registration timing, raw-payload identity
exclusion from test data, unpoisoned and oracle conditions, finite metrics, and exact
random-removal cost matching. Code, frozen specifications, and machine-readable result
artifacts are available at
\url{https://github.com/cdaneb/tda_data_poisoning_prevention}.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Supplied-material findings}
The supplied materials support partial forensic reconstruction but not exact reproduction.
The reconstructed Jupyter notebook pipeline uses $1\times1500$, threshold 0.3,
and 126 features. Its dataset labeled ``benign'' actually contains 21,000 normal plus 10,000 unmodified
attack-category rows sampled without a seed; 1,000 poison rows would be 3.125\%
of the combined set. Only 18 poisons from 11 DoS parents were delivered; all
change their parents, but none preserves the byte multiset like the controlled
attacks. No composition accompanies the saved DBSCAN silhouette (0.887854),
and missing poison data, generation code, and three cluster settings prevent
exact reproduction.

The aligned 280-feature TDA representation (TDA-280) Excel table has 140 constant coordinates, 24,925 unique
vectors, and 59,167 repeated members (74.069\%). Raw duplicates account for
80.587\% of those members; conflicting-label membership rises from 14.376\%
raw to 28.332\% after TDA. Intermediate states are unavailable, so the 19.413\%
post-raw share cannot be assigned to binarization versus persistence. The 126
Excel feature table has 63 constant coordinates and 85.387\% repeated members.
Alternating $H_1$ coordinates are constant in both $1\times1500$
feature-extraction configurations.

Separate fitting learns byte cuts 76.5 and 36.6 and changes all 18 poison
vectors (mean $L_2=531.500$), but DBSCAN labels are identical (ARI 1.0).
Both fitting procedures place all nine training poisons in the same 249-row mixed cluster.
Separate/shared silhouettes are 0.920/0.879; both remove zero poison. Thus the
fit confound is real at representation level but unconfirmed at decision level,
and the prespecified requirement for expansion to multiple seeds is not met.

Duplicate-safe classifier evaluation also changes interpretation. For TDA-280,
final-vector grouping lowers multiclass accuracy from 0.7958 to 0.7293 and
binary accuracy from 0.9581 to 0.8688. Raw bytes outperform TDA-280 under every
split protocol; their corresponding grouped accuracies are 0.8030 and 0.9080.
Large conflicting-label equivalence classes also increase between-seed
variation; complete split-protocol means and dispersions are retained in the
machine-readable supplement.

\subsection{Frame~A: exact matches arise before persistence summaries}
Frame~A shows that almost all repeated final-vector members first become
identical before persistence summaries are computed.
The final repeated-member fraction is $\meanstd{56.33}{0.68}$\%.
Raw-identical payloads create $\meanstd{50.80}{0.50}$\% of that collision mass
and binarization $\meanstd{46.68}{0.46}$\%; only
$\meanstd{2.52}{0.31}$\% first merges in persistence
(Fig.~\ref{fig:provenance}). Later processing stages introduce no new exact
matches. Because Frame~A permits attacks that leave the raw payload unchanged,
the raw-payload share is an upper bound on
the contribution of payload duplication under an attack that always modifies
its source; Frame~B, which requires every attack to modify its source, is the appropriate frame for
attack-specific claims.

In this frame, $\meanstd{42.28}{1.64}$\% of poison exactly matches an
unmodified vector, $\meanstd{56.24}{1.65}$\% is OPTICS noise, and only
$\meanstd{1.80}{0.51}$\% enters a pure-poison cluster. Equality matters, but
noise affects more poison.

Completing the strict-purity partition, $\meanstd{39.48}{1.73}$\% is non-noise
but shares a final vector with an unmodified row,
$\meanstd{2.48}{0.90}$\% is non-noise and coordinate-distinct but lies in a
mixed cluster, and the residual is $\meanstd{0.00}{0.00}$\%. Together with the
noise and captured terms above, these categories are exhaustive and sum to
100\% of poisoned rows in every seed.

\subsection{Frame~B: nine thresholds reduce exact feature matches}
The fixed nine-threshold representation removes most targeted
poison--unmodified equivalences.
On Frame~B, exact poison--unmodified matches fall from family means
2.60--4.32\% to 0.16--0.28\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:repair}), a 92.0--95.9\%
reduction in the four family means. The nine-threshold representation therefore repairs the
targeted equality mechanism.

\subsection{Frame~B: fixed OPTICS removes almost no poison}
Representation repair does not produce useful fixed-OPTICS separation.
At purity cutoffs 0.80 and above, the single-threshold and nine-threshold
representations remove only 0.00--0.20\%
and 0.00--0.64\% of poison, respectively, with zero unmodified removal. Poison
noise rises from 85.2--87.5\% to 98.7--99.3\%, unmodified noise from about
37.1\% to 54.5--54.6\%, and cluster count falls from 139.6--142.4 to
107.6--108.8. The nine-threshold representation distinguishes previously
identical vectors but fragments the density structure used by OPTICS; reducing
exact feature matches does not create poison-dominant clusters. At the
looser $q>0.50$ cutoff, every matched-cost difference is negative
($-1.88$, $-0.60$, $-1.52$, and $-1.56$ points across the four families).

Unmodified removal is zero for the nine-threshold representation at every
cutoff and for the single-threshold baseline through $q>0.80$; baseline cost
reaches 0.064--0.108\% at $q>0.50$, where the cost-matching constraint first
becomes active. Density-based cluster formation, not coordinate equality, is
the operative OPTICS obstacle.

\subsection{Novelty detectors identify poison that fixed OPTICS does not}
Label-free novelty scores recover moderate signal when higher unmodified-row
removal rates are allowed.
All 40 exploratory experimental configurations pass prespecified validity
checks. At the nominal 5\% unmodified-row removal target,
kNN captures 14.02\% of poison at 4.83\% realized unmodified removal with 60
features and 17.79\% at 5.23\% with 540. Isolation Forest reaches
16.34\%/5.09\% and 18.95\%/5.35\%, respectively; kNN using the 540-feature
representation improves in every
family.

This gain depends on the removal target: all detector--representation
combinations capture only 0.29--3.42\% at nominal 0.1--1\% targets. LOF is
weak, and the one-class SVM using the 60-feature baseline removes 21.74\% of
held-out unmodified rows at the nominal 5\% target.
Thus label-free scores recover signal discarded by fixed OPTICS, but do not
solve detection at low removal rates. Detectors were prespecified and are all
reported; this discovery result does not establish independent generalization.

\subsection{Confirmation rejects a general nine-threshold advantage}
Independent confirmation rejects a general removal advantage for the
nine-threshold representation.
Table~\ref{tab:confirmation} gives capture, realized cost, and paired
differences at the prespecified 5\% removal target. For the primary unseen-seed
UNSW claim, P1, P3, and P4 hold: the IF-540-minus-IF-60 capture difference is
$+0.69$ points, IF-540 capture is 14.68\%, and unmodified
removal is 4.72\%. P2 fails because the interval ($-0.66$ to $+1.98$) crosses
zero; the primary claim therefore fails on P2 alone. Transfer fails
independently: T1 holds at 5.04\%, but T2 fails because matched-size CICIDS capture
falls by 1.01 points; T3 also fails because large-scale CICIDS removal is 6.98\%.

\subsection{The preregistered downstream claim fails on magnitude}
The primary downstream claim fails because two effects miss their prespecified practical thresholds.
On matched-size UNSW, mean malicious recall is 0.99589 for clean (unpoisoned)
training, 0.97851
when poisoned, 0.98004 after equal-cost random removal, 0.98678 after IF-60
filtering, and 0.98682 after IF-540 filtering. Poisoning harm is therefore
1.74 percentage points (95\% hierarchical bootstrap CI 1.42--2.04), below the
prespecified 2-point mean threshold despite excluding zero. IF-60 recovers 0.83
points (0.67--0.97), also below its 1-point threshold. These two criteria fail.

The two remaining criteria pass (Fig.~\ref{fig:downstream-primary}). IF-60
exceeds recall after equal-cost random removal by 0.67 points (0.50--0.82),
showing that its benefit is
not explained by deletion alone. Its benign FPR is 1.11 points below the clean-training condition
($-1.42$ to $-0.83$), comfortably satisfying the requirement that filtering
not materially increase benign false positives. The
filter recovers 47.61\% of the poisoning-induced recall loss, but the
conjunctive four-criterion operational-benefit claim is not supported.

\subsection{Secondary evidence localizes utility and transfer failure}
The rows selected for removal explain the UNSW effect. At the primary setting, IF-60
removes 13.99\% of poisons while discarding 1.03\% of unmodified rows; matched
random removal eliminates only 5.16\% of poisons at exactly that unmodified
cost. Here and in Fig.~\ref{fig:downstream-generalization}, poison removal uses
all poisoned rows as the denominator, whereas unmodified cost uses the entire
unmodified training population. The same IF-60 and IF-540 removed-row counts
are 5.19\% and 4.72\%, respectively, of the held-out unmodified suspect rows
reported in Table~\ref{tab:confirmation}. IF-540 removes 14.68\% of poisons at
0.94\% cost, but changes unmodified-
test malicious recall by only $+0.003$ points relative to IF-60 (CI
$-0.228$ to $+0.231$). It improves recall on independently transformed
malicious copies by 1.85 points (0.34--3.53) while increasing benign FPR by
0.57 points (0.17--1.02) relative to IF-60. Thus the nine-threshold
representation changes performance on transformed malicious test copies
without improving the primary endpoint.

The pattern does not transfer (Fig.~\ref{fig:downstream-generalization}). On
matched-size CICIDS, poisoning harm is 4.54 points, but IF-60 recovery is only 0.22
points (0.12--0.31) and its advantage over random is effectively zero
($-0.15$ to $+0.15$). In the large-scale CICIDS experiment, harm is 3.17 points and IF-60 recovery
is 0.07 points ($-0.05$ to $+0.21$). At their respective 5\% settings, IF-60
removes only 1.38\% and 1.24\% of CICIDS poisons, fewer than the 5.49\% and
6.68\% removed randomly at matched cost. IF-540 is significantly worse than
random on both CICIDS populations. These registered secondary analyses explain,
but cannot rescue, the failed primary claim.

\section{Discussion}
The evidence forms a five-level evaluation framework: reproduction,
representation, cluster separability, label-free removal, and downstream
classifier utility. The supplied pipeline is partly reproducible; the
nine-threshold representation
passes its representation objective; fixed OPTICS fails at separation;
detectors fitted and calibrated on trusted unmodified data recover moderate
absolute signal; and the registered
classifier study finds a small, UNSW-specific benefit that does not meet the
operational decision rule. Success at one level does not establish the next.

The supplied $1\times1500$ implementation and controlled $30\times50$
reconstruction are different experimental objects. The former exposes
constant $H_1$ features, batch-fit confounding, and exact duplicates appearing
across training and test splits. In the latter, 97.48\% of repeated
final-vector members first acquire an exact match before persistence summaries
are computed, and the nine-threshold representation removes most
single-threshold matches. Fixed OPTICS still lacks poison-dominant density
structure. Fewer collisions prove the targeted information loss was reduced, not that
poison became separable. Poison capture from detectors calibrated on trusted
unmodified data identifies fixed OPTICS as a
downstream bottleneck, while confirmation shows that reducing exact collisions
does not reliably improve a different detector.

The downstream outcome is neither a successful operational claim nor a null
result. On UNSW, the positive filter-versus-random interval demonstrates a
TDA-specific selection effect, and the filter recovers nearly half of the lost
recall without increasing benign FPR. However, poisoning itself misses the
prespecified 2-point materiality threshold and recovery misses 1 point. Preserving
those thresholds prevents precise but small changes from being promoted to an
operational claim after outcomes are known.

The six-training-condition design also prevents two misleading interpretations. First,
poisoning lowers benign FPR from 4.25\% to 2.61\% while lowering malicious
recall, so aggregate accuracy alone can conceal the security-relevant tradeoff.

Second, deletion alone does not explain the UNSW recovery because matched
random removal is substantially weaker. Yet more poison removal is not
sufficient either: IF-540 has slightly better removal selectivity and stronger
transformed-attack recall than IF-60, but indistinguishable primary recall.

Transfer is the clearest limitation. Both CICIDS populations suffer larger
poisoning harm than UNSW, leaving a larger possible recall recovery, but the TDA sanitizer removes
fewer poisons than its random comparator and yields no useful filter-specific
recall gain. The failure is therefore not caused merely by a weak CICIDS attack.
The learned topological anomaly ordering is useful for this UNSW construction,
not a portable signature of poisoned packet payloads.

Limitations include incomplete author poison data/settings, a single-seed
18-poison fit diagnostic, and two packet-level derivatives rather than original
flow tables. The large-scale CICIDS experiment covers only transpositions; the
other experiments use matched-size populations. \emph{Unmodified} includes attack-category traffic, novelty
calibration assumes trusted data, and the attacks preserve byte multisets but
are not validated as functional network packets. Downstream evidence uses one
binary raw-byte random forest, one poisoning objective in which manipulated
malicious rows receive benign training labels, fixed training sizes, and two
calibration removal targets; it does not establish operational network
intrusion detection system (NIDS) impact.
The evaluation compares the TDA sanitizer against exactly cost-matched random
removal and an oracle, but not against representation-agnostic poison filters
such as spectral signatures or activation clustering; those comparisons would
situate the observed UNSW-specific enrichment against an established baseline
and remain open.

\section{Conclusion}
The supplied materials support forensic reconstruction of the published
pipeline, not its exact reproduction; the negative findings reported here
therefore constrain this reconstruction and the artifacts as delivered.
A fixed nine-threshold representation repairs most targeted exact collisions,
but neither fixed OPTICS nor independent confirmation supports a general
540-feature removal advantage. The preregistered downstream study closes the
remaining utility question: on UNSW, IF-60 removes poison more selectively than
matched random deletion and recovers 0.83 recall points, but the poisoning and
recovery magnitudes miss their prespecified practical thresholds. The
all-criteria claim
therefore fails. On CICIDS, filter-specific benefit disappears or reverses.

Topological filtering can provide a measurable dataset-specific signal without
providing reliable operational value. Future claims should preserve provenance,
use test sets that exclude exact training-payload identities and cost-matched
controls, separate statistical from
practical significance, and require cross-dataset evidence before treating TDA
poison filtering as a deployable defense.
